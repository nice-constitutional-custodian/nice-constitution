Inherited Infrastructure — Constitutional Constraints

This section defines the constitutional constraints imposed by inherited infrastructure in the NICE ecosystem.

NICE does not exist in a vacuum. Human participation is always mediated by physical compute, networks, power, ownership, jurisdiction, and failure modes. These conditions shape who can act, who can see, who can exclude, and what can fail — regardless of intent or governance design.

For NICE’s constitutional protections to hold, these inherited layers must satisfy explicit requirements.

These are not optimizations, preferences, or best practices.
They are structural conditions required to prevent silent capture, invisible gatekeeping, or erosion of human agency.

⸻

What “Inherited Infrastructure” Means

“Inherited infrastructure” refers to layers of reality that NICE does not create or control, but upon which all participation depends:
	•	Physical compute and execution environments
	•	Internet and transport protocols
	•	Physical network substrate (power, routing, connectivity)

These layers are inherited, not negotiated.
They cannot be wished away by better software, governance, or standards.

The NICE Constitution therefore makes their constraints explicit.

⸻

Why These Constraints Are Constitutional

Without explicit requirements at the infrastructure layer:
	•	Authority can shift silently through routing, hosting, or access control
	•	Custody can be undermined by opaque ownership or centralized execution
	•	Participation can be shaped by network position rather than consent
	•	Exit can become theoretical rather than real

The NICE Constitution treats these risks as constitutional failures, not operational issues.

Infrastructure is not neutral.
Control, power, and exclusion live here first.

⸻

Scope of This Section

This directory defines declarative constitutional requirements, not implementations.

It specifies:
	•	What conditions must hold
	•	What behaviors must be prevented
	•	What properties must remain inspectable and contestable

It does not specify:
	•	Vendors, providers, or architectures
	•	Performance targets or optimizations
	•	Preferred technologies or protocols
	•	Business or deployment models

Those belong to platform design and standards — not the constitution.

⸻

Declaration Model

Each file in this directory is a constitutional declaration describing one inherited infrastructure domain.

Declarations:
	•	Are normative, not descriptive
	•	Define constraints, not execution logic
	•	Apply regardless of implementation choice
	•	Are enforceable only through downstream governance and attestation

They follow the same constitutional pattern used elsewhere in NICE:
	•	Explicit constraints
	•	Clear “must not” boundaries
	•	Defensive posture against misinterpretation

⸻

Constitutional Invariants

Across all inherited infrastructure layers, the following invariants must hold:
	•	No silent gatekeeping — authority and control must be declared
	•	Participants operate as peers — no privileged network positions
	•	Resilience is mandatory — failure is assumed, not exceptional
	•	Stewardship is required — infrastructure carries responsibility

If these invariants are violated, constitutional protections cannot be guaranteed — regardless of higher-level governance.

⸻

Relationship to Other Constitutional Sections

Inherited infrastructure constraints:
	•	Inform eligibility and conformance evaluation
	•	Bound where execution may safely occur
	•	Shape what governance can legitimately permit
	•	Prevent hidden power from bypassing declared intent

They do not override:
	•	Human authority
	•	Declarative mediation
	•	Collective governance rules

They constrain how those principles may be realized in the physical world.

⸻

Summary

NICE makes inherited infrastructure explicit so that power cannot hide beneath abstraction.

Human agency depends on more than intent and rules —
it depends on the ground beneath them.

This section ensures that ground is visible, contestable, and constitutionally constrained.
